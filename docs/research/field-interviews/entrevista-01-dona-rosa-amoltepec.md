# 📋 Field Pain Validation Sheet: [INTERVIEW #01]

## 1. General Info
- **Community:** San Cristóbal Amoltepec, Oaxaca
- **Artisan:** Doña Rosa (62 years old, backstrap weaver)
- **Native Language:** Tu'un Savi (Mixteco) - speaks basic Spanish
- **Interview Date:** 2026-10-01
- **Researcher:** Open Hub TecNM / Raíz Protocol

## 2. The Economic Pain & Injustice (Real Numbers)
- **Craft piece:** Traditional wool rebozo dyed with natural cochineal.
- **Time invested:** 3 weeks of daily work (~90 manual hours).
- **Out-of-pocket material cost:** $450 MXN (raw wool, cochineal, alum).
- **Price paid by the "Coyote" (Middleman):** $800 MXN.
- **Estimated resale value by the middleman in Oaxaca / Mexico City:** $3,800 to $4,500 MXN.
- **Fair price requested by the artisan:** $2,200 MXN.

### Economic calculation
- Net income received by the artisan: $800 MXN - $450 MXN = $350 MXN net.
- Effective hourly pay: approximately $3.80 MXN/hour.
- Value captured by the intermediary: approximately +375% to +500% above what the weaver receives.

## 3. Specific Abuses Reported
- "The coyote takes 5 rebozos on credit, promises to pay at the end of the month, and often takes 3 months to pay."
- "If a piece has a tiny natural thread variation, the coyote uses it as an excuse to discount half the agreed price."
- "Tourists in town bargaining ('regateo'): people want to pay $300 pesos for something that took a month to weave."
- "Sometimes I finish the piece and still do not know when I will receive the payment, even though the buyer already sold it in the city."

## 4. Cultural Plagiarism
- Factory prints from Puebla are taking traditional Amoltepec rain and mountain patterns and selling them as "handmade Mixtec crafts" in tourist markets.
- The artisan explained that copied designs lose the symbolic and ancestral meaning of the original pattern, while the original maker receives no recognition or compensation.
- This is a direct threat to both income and cultural heritage.

## 5. Audio Testimony in Tu'un Savi
- **Audio file:** `audio_testimonies/ENT-01-dona-rosa-amoltepec.m4a` (1:12 min)
- **Spanish translation:**
  > "My grandmother taught me to weave on the waist loom when I was 10. Each symbol represents the rain and cornfield. We do not want middlemen to get rich while we cannot even afford thread. We want our work to be recognized, paid fairly, and not copied by machines that erase our story."

## 6. Key Validation Findings
1. The artisan invests substantial labor and capital, but the coyote captures most of the value.
2. Payment delays and arbitrary discounts are a recurring mechanism of exploitation.
3. Cultural patterns are being imitated by industrial production without attribution or compensation.
4. The lack of a traceable origin certificate prevents direct access to fair markets and ethical buyers.

## 7. Conclusion
This interview validates the need for a digital origin passport and direct market access that protect indigenous artisans from predatory intermediaries, guarantee a fair negotiated price, and preserve the cultural integrity of their designs.
